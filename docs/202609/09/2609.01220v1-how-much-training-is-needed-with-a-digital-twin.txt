# How Much Training is Needed with a Digital Twin? 

Ahmad Bazzi, Marwa Chafii 

Abstract —The following paper addresses how much pilot training is needed when a digital twin (DT) of the wireless radio channel is available to aid a wireless communication system with a channel estimation task. The DT of a wireless channel is widely expected to reduce the pilot overhead of channel estimation, following the informal rule that “the more accurate the twin, the fewer pilots are needed.” This trade-off, however, has only ever been demonstrated empirically and never quantified. We close this gap by treating the DT as a complementary measurement of the channel that the receiver fuses with its pilot observations in the physical world. Consequently, fusing the physical and digital worlds through the best linear unbiased estimator, we derive a DT-aided Cram´ er-Rao bound, and from it a pilot-equivalence law 

that converts DT fidelity into an equivalent number of training symbols. For a biased twin unknown to the estimator, we obtain the exact mismatch threshold beyond which trusting the DT is worse than ignoring it. We quantify how much training is needed with the DT to attain a desired mean square error on channel estimation. Particular cases are discussed to tell when training in the physical world can be completely bypassed. We finally translate these results into a block-fading achievable rate whose optimal training length is the unique root of a single equation, and identify the DT fidelity above which pilot training can be dispensed with altogether. Extensive numerical results corroborate closed-form expression and reveal that the value of a DT is largest at finite signal-to-noise ratio and vanishes in both the low- and high-SNR limits. 

Index Terms —Digital twin, channel estimation, pilot overhead, channel state information, 6G. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ORIGINATING in the manufacturing and aerospace com-munities two decades ago [1], and in particular within the United States Air Force Research Laboratory, a digital twin (DT) is a virtual replica of a physical entity [2]– [4] that is kept synchronized with it through a continuous exchange of measurements and predictions [5], [6]. In wireless communications, the DT has become a pillar of the 6Gvision, which contributes in maintaining a live model of the radio environment, traffic, and hardware, and serving as a risk free sandbox in which the network can be designed and optimized without experimenting on the live system [7]– [9]. The DT can help 6G wireless technologies enable new applications such as autonomous driving, indoor localization [10], and environmental monitoring and imaging [11]–[15], in addition to integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) [16]. Moreover, a DT can also be used as a simulation environment that goes beyond ISAC and can be extended for more complex tasks with different multimodal wireless networks [17], where communications and radio-frequency are

complemented with additional modalities, such as cameras [18]. However, the usefulness of any given DT is governed by a single quantity, which is the DT’s fidelity , namely how faithfully the virtual model tracks the physical one. This has been formalized both as model uncertainty induced by limited data [5], [19] and, information theoretically, as a joint function of model quality and the information exchanged between the physical and digital worlds [6]. At the network level, DTs support zero touch 6G manage-ment and resource orchestration [20] across edge, aerial, en-able security [21], [22], vehicular, and industrial systems [23]– [30], integrated sensing and communications [1], [31]–[33] and edge computing links [1], [34]–[37], and are increasingly driven by generative artificial intelligence [7]–[9], with recent analysis bounding the deployed performance of a DT trained policy by a formal twin to reality discrepancy [38], [39]. Our interest, however, lies one layer down, which is within the DT of the wireless radio channel. 

The DT of a wireless radio channel can be built by importing a 3 dimensional model of some site-specific en-vironment, assigning electromagnetic materials to its surfaces, and possibly synthesize the channel through possible ray tracing approaches [40]–[42]. On this basis, the DTs can fuse ray tracing with learning for multi-spectrum propagation modeling [40], map user positions to statistical channel state information (CSI) via some diffusion model [43], counter channel aging by generative data augmentation [44], track reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) channels [45], [46] with physics informed neural networks [47], [48], and place ray tracing directly in the vehicular simulation process [49]. 

Since the rendered channel rests on an imperfect geometry and material description, however, a DT never matches reality exactly; this residual sim-to-real gap, embedded in geometry and material mismatch, is one fundamental challenge in what caps fidelity [5], [6]. But even with this gap, the DT has proven to be a powerful tool for lowering overhead of pilot based channel acquisition, where the overhead reduction will be most useful in the large antenna array regime and millimeter wave bands envisioned for 6G, because pilots are expensive due to a multitude of reasons: (i) pilots will create overhead in learning the channel, and training too much will eventually lower the achievable rate when communicating within a fixed coherence interval, 

(ii ) reusing pilots across cells introduces the so-called pilot contamination, and (iii ) and the cost of beam sweeping grows with the number of antenna elements [41], which becomes more dramatic in 6G as massive multiple-input and multiple-output (MIMO) is expected to be used [50]. A first body of work exploits the fact that a site-specific DT can generate its own channel data. Such synthetic CSI has been used to train feedback and beam management networks, which then arXiv:2609.01220v1 [eess.SP] 1 Sep 2026 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON WIRELESS COMMUNICATIONS 2

require far less real world data than models trained from measurements alone [42], [51]–[53]. A second body of work uses the DT rendered channel directly, which is more optimistic as more confidence is placed on the DT itself. Here, the channel produced by the DT drives the precoder through some over-the-air channel impulse response inference [41], and so statistical CSI can be obtained from the user positions without transmitting any pilots at all [43]. Across the literature, an informal statement reads as follows: 

“the more accurate the DT, the fewer pilots/training are needed.” [41]–[43], [54], [55]. It is, however, only ever demonstrated empirically for a particular system. It has never been quantified, to the best of the author’s knowl-edge. For instance, the DT approach in [42] contains the relevant site-specific information, which was stated to require less feedback overhead to achieve the same or even better performance than a generic dataset. Furthermore, [43] does not require any pilots so that their framework can generate statistical CSIs from a large number of positions, however achieving satisfactory results. Also, [55] argues that if the digital replica is adequately accurate, the real world channel acquisition can even be bypassed. Moreover, [54] infers that a DT can predict the information about the wireless channel in the real environment even by eliminating the channel acquisition overhead. Also, [41] provides numerical results showing the accuracy of the proposed channel DT without CSI acquisition. Meanwhile, [56] highlighted that a key challenge in the application of DT technology is the channel simulators requirements on the accuracy of the 3D DT. In retrospect, one may even wonder, how many pilots is a DT worth? how does that worth scale with some DT’s uncertainty about the environment, and when does trusting a DT become worse than using no DT at all? or even more broadly, how much training is needed with a digital twin ? 

Classical training theory quantifies pilots against signal to noise ratio (SNR) and/or number of antennas in the MIMO case [57], while existing notions of DT fidelity [6], [38] do not translate into pilots. We intend to close this gap by modeling the DT as supplemental information on top of the training observations in the physical world, which serves an additional measurement of the wireless channel. > •

DT measurement model. We treat the DT as a com-plementary measurement of the wireless channel, avail-able to the receiver on top of the training observations acquired in the physical world (PW). We then establish the conditions under which this measurement admits a Gaussian model, by invoking the Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem (CLT) over the path errors of the DT. We further show that both moments of the Gaussian model are dictated by the electromagnetic properties the DT assigns to the surfaces. In particular, we simulate the DT error from the Fresnel coefficients and the full polari-metric path gain over a deployment ensemble, and we demonstrate that a wrong material assumption increases the variance, whereas a slowly varying path phase turns into a bias that turns out to be frequency-dependent.  

> •

Pilot equivalence of a DT and its mismatch threshold. 

We fuse the PW and the digital world through the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) estimator and derive a DT aided Cram´ er–Rao bound (CRB) that interpolates continuously between pilot only estimation, obtained for an uninformative DT, and genie aided CSI, obtained for a perfect DT. Thanks to this expression, we derive a pilot equivalence law that converts the precision of the DT into an equivalent number of training symbols, thereby giving a measure for DT fidelity, together with the training length needed to reach a target mean square error (MSE). For the case of a biased DT that is unknown to the estimator, we obtain the exact mismatch threshold beyond which trusting the DT is worse than ignoring it. Beyond that threshold, the DT is worth a negative number of pilots, an effect we refer to as unlearning of the PW.  

> •

Achievable rate and optimal training length. We translate the above estimation results into an achievable rate over a block fading channel, for both an unbiased and a biased DT, and we derive the training length that maximizes this rate as the unique root of a single equation. Thanks to these expressions, we recover the pilot-only and the genie-aided rates as limiting cases, and we identify the DT fidelity above which training can be dispensed with altogether. Finally, we present extensive numerical results that corrob-orate every closed form expression and unveil some important insights, i.e.  

> •

An incorrect material assumption within the DT can increase the DT uncertainty (or the DT variance) by a factor of 3.8 when the surfaces are rendered as a light dielectric instead of a near metal, while the very same error turns into a bias at lower frequencies.  

> •

The number of pilots a DT replaces decays inversely with the operating SNR in the PW for the unbiased case and is further reduced through a factor that depends on the bias as well as the amount of training used for the biased case, according to the pilot-equivalence law.  

> •

Training in the PW shrinks as the DT becomes more accurate, to the point where no training is needed at all, and it increases once the DT is biased or less accurate. For a coherence block of 30 symbols and a DT variance of 0.05 , an unbiased DT attains 3 bits /s/Hz with a single training symbol, whereas a bias of 0.3 needs 3 training symbols to deliver 2.75 bits /s/Hz , which is exactly the best rate attained with no DT at all, and a bias of 

0.55 needs about 4 training symbols while delivering only 2.5 bits /s/Hz . An unbiased DT operating at 10dB 

requires no training at all to achieve a channel MSE error of 10 −2 as long as the DT variance does not exceed 10 −3. 

> •

The value of a DT in terms of achievable rate is maxi-mized (when compared to the achievable rate of no DT) at a moderate SNR and vanishes in both the low and the high SNR limits. For instance, when the variance of the IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON WIRELESS COMMUNICATIONS 3

DT is 0.05 , the optimal SNR for maximum value of a DT in terms of achievable rate occurs at 2.5, and that SNR decreases with increasing bias. For a highly biased DT, e.g. 0.35 , there exists SNR regions where the DT is more harmful over a mid SNR range. 

Organization : The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II introduces the system model, casting the DT as a complementary Gaussian measurement of the channel. Section III fuses this measurement with the physical-world pilots through the best linear unbiased estimator and derives the DT-aided CRB, the pilot-equivalence law, and, for a biased twin, the mismatch threshold and the associated unlearning regime. Section IV translates these estimation results into a block-fading achievable rate and obtains the optimal training length in closed form. Section V presents numerical results that corroborate the closed-form expressions and quantify the value of a DT across signal-to-noise ratio, DTvariance, as well as the DT bias. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper.

The figure depicts the fundamental tradeoff in wireless communications, where transmitting more pilots improves the channel estimate but cannibalizes the time available to actually send data, hence creating an optimal operation point for training length for maximum achievable rate. For a traditional system operating without a DT (labeled as Hassibi-Hochwald bound [57]), we observe that a significant chunk of the coherence block must be wasted on training just to reach optimal capacity. In particular, the optimal training length is Tτ ≃ 3 symbols to maximize the achievable rate, which in this case attains about 2.75 bits /s/Hz . However, as the DT’s information becomes more precise (smaller σ2DT ), not only reduces the optimal training length, but also increases the achievable rate. For instance, when σ2DT = 0 .05 , the optimal training length is about Tτ ≃ 1 symbols, and achieves a capacity of 3 bits /s/Hz . This means that an accurate DT inherently reduces the physical overhead required for channel estimation, freeing up more symbols for data payload, which then leaves positive reverberation effects on the achievable rate. In other words, a more accurate DT shortens the optimal training and increases the achievable rate towards the perfect-CSI case, whereas an uninformative DT coincides with the pilot only rate of Hassibi and Hochwald [57]. In this paper, we addressed the question of how much training is needed with a DT by modeling the DT as a complementary Gaussian measurement of the wireless chan-nel, whose bias and variance follow from a Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem over the DT’s errors and are governed by the electromagnetic materials assigned to the environment. Moreover, using the DT measurement along with physical world pilots via the best linear unbiased estimator, we derived a DT-aided Cram´ er-Rao bound, a pilot-equivalence law that converts DT fidelity into an equivalent number of pilots, and a bias mismatch threshold beyond which the DT becomes de-structive and unlearns the physical world. Casting the analysis as a block-fading achievable rate, we obtained the optimal training length in closed form and characterized when pilot training can be dispensed with altogether. These results quantify the common claim that a more accu-rate DT requires fewer pilots. We have shown that an incorrect material assumption within the DT inflates its variance σ2DT by a factor of about 3.8 through a ray tracing example, whereas the same error turns into a frequency-dependent bias once the path phases stay correlated across the deployment. Moreover, the number of pilots a DT replaces decays inversely with the operating SNR, hence the value of a DT, in terms of achievable rate, is largest at a moderate SNR and vanishes in both the low and high SNR limits. Future directions include extending the framework to DTs whose bias is partially known to the estimator, to massive MIMO and wideband millimeter wave channels, and to online DT calibration that keeps the fidelity, and hence the pilot savings, high as the environment evolves.
